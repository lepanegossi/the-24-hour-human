05The heart of the story20:00 · Night

The double shift

The double shift, and one stubborn question: why is it almost always women? There's a second shift, the invisible one: cooking, cleaning, raising children, caring for elders. It never shows on a payslip, it sits outside policy indicators, and it stays out of the conversation. Around the world it lands overwhelmingly on women. In India, women do about five more hours of unpaid work every single day than men. Turkey, Portugal and Mexico aren't far behind, while only the Nordics come close to sharing it evenly. And the penalty is double: those same women also get less time to rest. In Portugal and Italy, men enjoy almost an hour and a half more leisure every single day. Calling women strong and empowered doesn't erase those extra hours. This is the part of the day the economy never counts, the part that shapes millions of lives most, and it's long past time we talked about it as a society.

An hour is a policy choice

Nothing in this data is natural law. Every gap you just scrolled through was decided by someone: a working week, a school timetable, who is expected to cook. The 1,440 minutes are fixed. Almost nothing else is. Here are the three things this dataset can actually show about where the hours come back from.

Where the data comes from

  • OECD Time Use Database — the five slices of the day, split by sex, for people aged 15–64. Minutes per day, pulled from the SDMX dataflow DSD_TIME_USE@DF_TIME_USE.
  • World Bank — GDP per capita, PPP (indicator NY.GDP.PCAP.PP.CD), used as the income measure.
  • Our World in Data — life satisfaction (Cantril ladder), annual working hours, average effective retirement age, and leisure by sex.

How to read these numbers

  • Every "per day" figure is an average over everyone, and over every day. OECD divides a country's total time by its whole adult population — people in paid work and people out of it — and across all seven days, holidays included. So paid work per day is not the length of a working day: Italy’s 2h57 is a whole country’s paid hours spread across every adult and every date, and Japan’s 6h08 at the top of the range is the same calculation, not a longer shift.
  • The five categories sum to 24 hours, so every chart here is a share of one real day. The one exception is "a year of work", which is counted per worker rather than per person, and so cannot be compared with the daily figures.
  • Sleep is not separable. It sits inside personal care together with meals and hygiene, which is why we never claim a sleep figure.
  • Commuting is not its own category. It falls under "getting around", with whatever else the surveys could not place.
  • Reference years differ by country, each being the most recent available, so this is a snapshot and not a time series. The extract behind this page does not carry the year per country, which is why no chart is dated.
  • 20 of the 35 countries carry OECD’s “definition differs” flag on at least one category, so a gap of a few minutes between two countries is not necessarily a real one.
  • Coverage is 35 OECD and partner countries. No South American country is in the time-use database, which is why the map has a hole over the continent.
  • The 2050 screen is an illustrative model, not a forecast: it reads the relationship between income and time across countries and applies it to one of them.
